// THE GHOST EMPIRE

Yugoslavia dissolved in the nineties, but the infrastructure it left behind didn't disappear — it just stopped being maintained. In the summer of 2020, we drove a loop through six successor states and former satellite countries, following the decay: cooling towers, ideological monuments, military hilltops abandoned when the Cold War ended, and facilities that outlasted the states that built them by thirty years.

Ten days, a Czech-plated VW, four people. We left Liberec in early July — in the narrow window when Europe had briefly reopened after the first lockdown and before it shut again — and headed south to find what was left.

// HUNGARY — THE COAL PLANT

HU · Oroszlány · Vértes Power Plant

The Vértes power plant ran on brown coal dug from the ground a few kilometres away. Three concrete cooling towers visible from the motorway — the kind of structure you see from thirty kilometres on a clear day. Hungary was closing its last lignite plants under EU pressure; Vértes had just gone cold.

Inside, the scale of industrial abandonment is different from buildings. The turbine halls are cathedral-sized, the pipe runs go for hundreds of metres, the transformer bays hold equipment that couldn't be moved without a crane. Everything coated in the same grey-brown of lignite dust. Everything still — the kind of stillness that only exists in places that used to be very loud.

// SERBIA — BELGRADE

RS · Belgrade · City centre

Belgrade was one night and most of a morning. Kalemegdan above the Sava confluence, the blocky socialist architecture of New Belgrade across the river. A city that could fill a separate page; we gave it twelve hours and moved on.

// BULGARIA — BUZLUDZHA & SOFIA

BG · Buzludzha · Monument House of the Bulgarian Communist Party

You see Buzludzha from the road as you climb toward the Shipka Pass — a concrete disc on the ridgeline, the size of a sports arena, sitting at 1441 metres with no visible reason to be there. The closer you get, the more it looks like something landed rather than was built.

The Monument House of the Bulgarian Communist Party was inaugurated in 1981. Fifty thousand people came to the ceremony. Eight years later the regime fell and the building was sealed. The exterior has been deteriorating since; the interior — the mosaics, the hammer-and-sickle floors, the propaganda murals — still technically in there, behind boarded-up entrances that open if you know which boards move.

BG · Sofia

Sofia afterward: wide Soviet boulevards, the Alexander Nevsky cathedral, yellow trams. A horse grazing on the abandoned tarmac of a parking lot outside the city.

// GREECE — THESSALONIKI: TRAIN GRAVEYARD

GR · Near Thessaloniki · OSE locomotive depot

Just off the main rail line south of Thessaloniki, behind a chain-link fence with gaps in several places, the Greek railway operator OSE parked dozens of 1970s and 1980s passenger cars and left them. The network shrank after the financial crisis; the surplus rolling stock had nowhere to go. The cars stand in long rows, vegetation grown up through the floor panels, upholstery still in the seats. Blue sky above, rust below, nothing moving.

// GREECE — ATHENS: ELLINIKON

GR · Athens · Ellinikon Olympic Complex / UNHCR camp

The old Athens airport at Ellinikon closed in 2001 when the new one opened. Three years later, for the 2004 Olympics, the zone was repurposed — softball diamond here, field hockey pitch there, canoe slalom course dug into the tarmac. Then abandoned again after the closing ceremony.

The sports halls are open to the weather now, roof panels gone. In one of the field hockey buildings, a grey UNHCR banner hangs from a steel upright — the refugee camp was here from 2016, families in temporary shelter where Olympic athletes had competed two years before. The banner is the only thing that marks the transition.

// GREECE — ELEUSIS: SHIP GRAVEYARD

GR · Eleusis · Ship breaking bay

Twenty kilometres west of Athens, the bay at Eleusis is where ships end up when they can no longer justify the cost of fuel. Some are being actively broken apart at the yards on the shore; others just sit at anchor in the shallows, waiting.

One vessel had its gangway lowered — either forgotten or deliberately left. The bridge was intact: navigation consoles, chart tables, the view forward through angled windows to the rust-streaked bow. Life vests piled in the corner, still orange, still bright.

// GREECE — ACE HIGH NATO RADAR

GR · ACE High troposcatter relay station

The ACE High system was NATO's Cold War communications backbone — a chain of troposcatter relay stations stretching from Norway to Turkey that didn't require satellites or undersea cables. Each station bounced signals off the troposphere to the next link in the chain. The Greek station sits on a hilltop, the main dish — a lattice of aluminium rings large enough to walk under — still pointing at where the next station used to be.

Inside: equipment left in place. Filter cartridges on the floor. A wall of pinned photographs and documents in what was once the operations room — left by someone, for reasons that aren't obvious.

// ALBANIA — THE AMMUNITION FACTORY

AL · Poliçan · Uzina e Ndërtimit të Armëve (UNA)

Poliçan is a town that exists because of a factory, and a factory that no longer exists. Under Enver Hoxha's regime of total self-sufficiency, Albania built its own arms industry in this mountain valley, supplied with Chinese technology and Chinese technicians. At its peak, the UNA complex employed most of the town.

The Chinese connection shows in what was left behind: precision instruments in wooden carrying cases with Chinese labels, calibration tools, measurement apparatus from Shanghai factories — things that couldn't be taken when the contracts ended in 1978, when Albania broke with China as sharply as it had broken with Yugoslavia and the Soviet Union before it. Furnaces for heat-treating metal components, bunkers cut into the valley walls, assembly halls where the ceiling lights still hang. A shelf in one room holds a cassette player and a transistor radio, things left by whoever came here last.

The complex is enormous and almost entirely accessible. The valley keeps it quiet.

// CROATIA — SOUTH COAST

HR · Dalmatia · South coast

After Albania the Dalmatian coast is a different kind of extreme. Pine forest straight down to turquoise water, limestone pale in the afternoon heat. We swam. After ten days of industrial archaeology, the act of getting into clear water felt disproportionately good.

The drive back north — petrol stations every hundred kilometres, a barren landscape that doesn't care which country it's in. You cross from Croatia into Bosnia without noticing, then Slovenia, then back into Czech Republic. The cooling towers at home look smaller than the ones in Hungary.
