Croatia
& Bosnia

Nine days, two countries, zero hotel check-ins — chasing the ruins of a state that dissolved before anyone could sweep them up. The Adriatic coast as a back road to forgotten Yugoslavia.

Countries
Croatia, BiH
Dates
4 – 12 July 2018
Days
9
Mode
Road trip

People always ask us why we sleep in ruins. The honest answer: we can't afford hotels. Somewhere between the cheap excuse and the truth, there's a story about Yugoslavia — a country that built monuments to its own permanence, then dissolved before anyone got the memo. We went to find what it left behind.

The former Yugoslavia is one of the stranger political grief-sites on earth. After Tito's death in 1980 the cracks widened slowly, then catastrophically. By 1991 Slovenia was gone, Croatia was burning, and Bosnia was heading for three years of a war so tangled that at various points everyone fought everyone. What they left — military airports, luxury hotels, partisan monuments, Olympic venues — is now a patchwork of ruins along coasts and mountains that tourists photograph for their pools and sunsets, mostly unaware of what's just inland.

// Across the Adriatic

The first stop was Petrova Gora, a 512-metre hill in the Croatian interior where a massive steel monument was built in the 1980s to honour partisan fighters from World War II. On 4 March 1990, just days before Croatia's first free election, 70,000 people gathered here for what started as a brotherhood rally and turned into a Serbian nationalist demonstration. The hill was primed. Within weeks the wars began. The monument still stands, stripped of its reflective panels, rusting in the forest.

Fifty kilometres south and underground: Željava. Designed from 1948, finished in 1968 at a cost of $6 million, the Željava Air Base was built into the mountain on the Croatian-Bosnian border — 3.5 km of tunnels, hangars for 100 aircraft, capable of operating autonomously for 30 days with the doors sealed. When the JNA (Yugoslav People's Army) was forced to abandon it in 1992, they mined it first. The Republika Srpska Krajina forces finished the job. What remains is a cavern you walk through with a head torch and the hairs standing up on your neck.

Further south along the Dalmatian coast: Hotel Marina Lučica near Primošten, opened 1971, over 700 rooms, once considered one of the most modern hotels in the Mediterranean — and famously open about being nudist. The war ended it by 1994. The nudist beach below it is apparently still in use. The hotel is not. Then Krvavica: a children's respiratory sanatorium on the coast, opened 1964, shuttered by the war in 1991. The state couldn't afford to renovate it and didn't want to sell it to a developer. So it just… stands there.

// Into Herzegovina

Kupari is the one that gets you. In 1920 a Czech entrepreneur built a spa and hotel called Grand in a small bay near Dubrovník — the first hotel in the area. Tito loved it. He added more: Hotel Gloričina (1962), Hotel Pelegrin (1963), Hotel Kupari (1978), Hotel Gloričina II (1980). The whole bay became a resort reserved for the military-political elite — up to 2,000 guests in the hotels, 4,000 more in the surrounding complex. On 1 October 1991 the siege of Dubrovnik began. The JNA looted everything that wasn't shelled, then dropped white phosphorus bombs on the hotels as a demonstration of force to the city nearby. The siege ended May 1992. Kupari hasn't been used since.

Mostar was the hardest to stand in. The old bridge — Stari Most — spanned the Neretva River since the 16th century, connecting the Catholic Croatian west bank with the Muslim Bosniak east bank. During the war 60 Croatian artillery pieces brought it down in November 1993. It has been rebuilt. The bullet holes in the walls haven't been filled — they're left as record. The city is beautiful and fractured in exactly the right ways to make you understand the phrase "never again" is a wish, not a guarantee.

"The ones who build monuments never imagine who'll be picking through them fifty years later."

From Mostar we tracked north to Sarajevo — the Olympic bobsled track from the 1984 Winter Games, overgrown and spray-painted, winding down from Trebević mountain. Hotel Igman and the ski jumps, used as NATO UNPROFOR positions during the siege, still pockmarked with shrapnel. The road between the airport and the hotel was called the most dangerous road in Europe during the war. We drove it at dusk and it was just a road. That's the strangest part: how quickly places go back to being just places. Until you look at the walls.

// North, Through Kumrovec

On the way back we stopped at Kumrovec, the village in northern Croatia where Josip Broz Tito was born and where, in 1975, a political school bearing his name was opened — one of the most prestigious in Yugoslavia. A new wing with 145 double rooms followed in 1981, a year after Tito's death. Through the 1980s it functioned as a political academy; by the end of the decade, as a military one too. During the war it housed refugees from Vukovar. The last residents left in 2003. Since then the building has sat empty — its corridors still, rooms scattered with newspapers and personal effects, the lecture hall's books exactly where someone left them in a hurry. A Chinese investor bought it in 2019 for two million euros with plans to turn it into a Tito museum. At the time of our visit it was just the quiet kind of abandoned that gets under your skin.

We also made a detour to Strbacki Buk, a waterfall on the Una River just inside the Bosnian border — a reminder that not everything the former Yugoslavia left behind is scarred. Some of it is just beautiful.
