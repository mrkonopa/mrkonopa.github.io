KOSOVOand its neighbours

Nine days and just under four thousand kilometres, from Liberec down to the Macedonian lakes and back up the Adriatic. A lead mine at Trepça, the fortress hill of Novo Brdo, a church left standing in a reservoir, and a salt works that runs to the horizon.

// HUNGARY — THE FIRST NIGHT

The train graveyard

Istvántelek · Budapest · HU

A railway works on the northern edge of Budapest, half still in service and half left to itself. In the abandoned half the glazed roof has come down in sheets, birches have grown up out of the inspection pits, and a brick water tower stands over the sidings. Between the halls the carriages have gone matt under decades of graffiti. We got in at the end of the day, when the light was coming through the broken roof in stripes.

// SERBIA — VOJVODINA

The Spitzer house

Beočin · RS

A manor under the Fruška Gora, built for the family that owned the cement works below it. The shell is intact enough to show what it was: round oculus windows over the arcade, a stone eagle still sitting on the gable, and in one upstairs room a painted ceiling in pink and gold that has come through the rain better than the floor underneath it has.

Jugoalat

Novi Sad · RS

A tool factory on the edge of Novi Sad and by a long way the biggest thing we walked into on this trip — offices with the paperwork still in the shelves, a canteen, then hall after hall of machinery pulled half to pieces for scrap. On a wall in the administration block hangs an enamel badge: JUGOALAT · 1935–1985, made for the fiftieth anniversary. The factory did not get another forty.

// KOSOVO — LEAD, SILVER AND MEMORY

Trepça

Stantërg · Mitrovica · XK

Lead, zinc and silver have come out of this valley since the Middle Ages, and the Trepça combine that grew up around it was once the largest industrial employer in Kosovo. What stands on the hillside now is a company town coming apart slowly: winding gear, a locomotive rusted onto its rails, ore chutes running down the slope, a chemical plant with its windows out. From the top you can see the whole works laid out in the trees, with the village roofs in among it.

Novo Brdo

XK

A bare hill east of Pristina with the stumps of a fortress on top and an excavation opened up around it. In the fourteenth century this was one of the richest mining towns in the Balkans — silver and argentiferous lead, its own mint, Saxon miners, Ragusan traders. What is there now is grass, a few courses of wall, and a very long view.

Pristina

XK

Three buildings in one afternoon, and no two of them agree about anything. The National Library “Pjetër Bogdani” under its domes and its steel net; the Catholic cathedral of Mother Teresa, still in scaffolding; and behind the university, the Orthodox church begun in the 1990s and never finished, standing in waist-high grass with its brick still raw.

Prekaz

XK

The Jashari family houses, kept as they were left in March 1998 — shot through from the ground to the roofline, the render gone in patches the size of doors, a steel frame now built over the top of them to keep the weather off. Behind, on the slope, the graves in a long row and a wide circular plaza laid out in front of them. It was the quietest place we stopped at, and the only one where turning up as a visitor felt like it needed explaining.

// NORTH MACEDONIA — TWO LAKES

The church in the reservoir

Mavrovo · MK

Sveti Nikola was left standing when the valley was dammed in the 1950s, and for much of the year the water is around it. We came in a dry summer and walked out to it over the lake bed: no roof, the arcades open to the sky, the plaster gone down to the stone, and a bell turret still on top of it.

Oteševo

Lake Prespa · MK

A whole holiday settlement on the west shore of Prespa, emptied and left standing: terraced concrete blocks stepped up the slope, a pine wood closing in on the car parks, corridors painted over with somebody’s murals, a sawtooth glass roof above what must have been a dining room. Hotel Evropa, the biggest of them, burned in 2004 and never reopened. Down on the shore the lake has pulled a long way back from where it is supposed to be.

The children’s hospital

Ohrid · MK

A low pavilion hospital above the lake road, closed and stripped — consulting rooms with the fittings torn off the walls, wooden shutters pulled to, a ladder left standing in a doorway, and that particular green-and-white institutional paint that the whole region seems to have been issued at once.

// MONTENEGRO — SALT, WATER AND CONCRETE

Solana Ulcinj

Ulcinj · ME

The largest place on the trip, and not a building at all. The Ulcinj salt works took its first harvest in 1935, went bankrupt in 2005 and lost the right to extract salt in 2011. What is left is roughly fifteen square kilometres of evaporation pans and feeder canals running dead straight to the horizon, with the industrial end of it stripped back to the steel: roof trusses standing over nothing, a hall swept clean with its shutters closed, the pumps gone. On the front of the administration block the Cyrillic still reads УПРАВА СОЛАНЕ У УЛЦИЊУ, with a bust on a plinth in the grass in front of it. The pans themselves have been taken over by birds; in the middle of the afternoon the only thing moving was one of them, high up over the water.

The R15 over Rumija

Lake Skadar · ME

Rather than the coast road we took the R15, which climbs out of the Ulcinj plain over the Rumija ridge and comes down towards Lake Skadar. From the top the whole lake is laid out underneath — the largest in the Balkans, shared with Albania — shallow, islanded and hazy with heat, and behind it the mountains going up in flat grey steps.

The unfinished hotel

Montenegrin coast · ME

Storey after storey of bare concrete frame above a bay, curved in plan, with the balcony slabs cantilevered out and nothing on them. Somebody clearly means to finish it: there is a tower crane standing in the middle of the building and lettering along one flank. From below it looks less like a ruin than like a thing paused.

The submarine pens

Bay of Kotor · ME

Tunnels cut into the rock at the mouth of the bay, wide enough to take a submarine and high enough to clear a conning tower, with a concrete quay down one side and the sea running in at the far end. The Yugoslav navy put them here to keep boats out of sight from the air. Inside they are cold and echoing and sprayed over near the entrances, and the way out is a white rectangle of Adriatic at the end of a very long dark room.
