Kyiv
& Ukraine

Eight days through Ukraine in the height of summer 2017 — Kyiv as the home base, and a rolling list of places that aren't on the tourist map. Soviet-era industry, empty corridors, long train rides east.

Country
Ukraine
Visited
27 Jul – 3 Aug 2017
Base
Kyiv
Approx. coords
50.44° N · 30.49° E

Five friends who met through airsoft — a hobby that consists of attempting to maim your friends with plastic pellets. We don't travel often, but when we do it tends to involve abandoned buildings, sleeping bags on concrete, and a great deal of talking about it afterwards. Ukraine 2017 was the first time we left the country for it. The reason we sleep in ruins, by the way, is not that it makes us interesting. It's that we can't afford normal hotels.

Kyiv is a city of violent contrasts. A 4-star hotel room costs around 250 CZK a night. The deepest metro station in the world — Arsenalnaya, 105 metres underground — sits a short walk from parliament. Czech trams run on the tracks. The cheapest beer on earth is on tap three streets from the Maidan, where in 2014 a trade-union building burned to the ground and the scorched facade still reads Svoboda je naše víra — Freedom is our faith. Nearly 80% of Ukrainians have never left the country. The minimum official wage is 3,000 hryvnia. People frequently don't reach it.

// Underground Kyiv

Below the city run three independent systems: sewage, underground rivers, and a storm-drain network for surface runoff. We went in through the third. The tunnels branch and stack — some descend tens of metres. In places they narrow to a crawl. Even if it rains heavily the water reaches waist height at most, but you need to watch the forecast: it's the underground rivers you have to worry about, not the tunnels themselves. Our guide was a local who runs expeditions to abandoned rocket silos in Kazakhstan and spent time in Fukushima (where the fine for unauthorised access is about 30,000 CZK per person, versus the organised Chernobyl tours). He charged a few euros a day and was worth every one of them. The fauna at the bottom included at least one representative waiting patiently in the dark.

// The Bridge

The Podilsko-Voskresensky Bridge was born the same year as me — 1993 — and has been under construction since. Its predicted completion date was 2021, which we also have in common. Height: approximately 140 metres. Access: through the hollow side beams. We set an alarm for the pre-dawn so we'd make the sunrise from the top. The climb up was demanding enough. The view from the deck — the city spreading out in every direction, the Dnipro below, a single construction worker who was presumably the foreman, site engineer, and security guard simultaneously — was worth every step. Some photos were taken for Facebook. It was visibly getting lighter.

// Chernobyl

The exclusion zone is divided into three rings by contamination level. In the outermost, a few original residents still live. The wildlife, paradoxically, thrives — radiation is apparently a smaller burden than people. We took a minibus from Kyiv main station (about an hour, documentary shown en route), passed the entrance monument at the town of Chernobyl, which predates the plant and gave it its name, and headed for Duga II — the Soviet over-the-horizon radar nicknamed "the Russian Woodpecker." Structure: 120 metres high, support towers at 150 m, total length 770 metres. It was meant to be the largest radar and communications jammer ever built. In practice it mostly jammed radio in the surrounding area. It cannot be demolished: the risk of ground vibration damaging the reactor is too high. So it stands.

Pripyat. The city built for the plant's workers was self-sufficient in every way — supermarket, cultural centre, sports hall, swimming pool (the famous one from the video games), a funfair that opened four days before the explosion. Gas masks in the school storeroom that were never collected. Shopping trolleys in the mall, because metals absorb radiation and nobody came back for them. The tourist paths through the city have been worn down by years of guided groups walking exactly the same routes — you can see the worn lines through the moss. Our guide suggested the obligatory carousel photo. Our guide was told no. Then everyone started climbing and she eventually agreed to take the picture.

"Zákaz vstupu jako by ani neexistoval." — No entry signs might as well not exist.

// The Carpathians

After Kyiv and the zone, a train east to the mountains. Ukrainian Carpathians: an overloaded backpack, temperatures around 45°C, and a trail marker system identical to the Czech one. We crossed Pjetros (second-highest peak in Ukraine) to reach Hoverla (highest). Between them you must first descend into the valley, because Ukraine has its own concerns without building cable cars for lazy Western tourists. We bought a watermelon from shepherds at the summit for American dollars. A man we met on the way down turned out to be Czech, part of a team that supplies the Dakar Rally — he was spending his holiday driving to the highest peaks in Eastern Europe with friends. The sheep were uncountable. The water was much colder than expected. The motorike lasted approximately 200 metres before breaking down, which the owner considered fair exchange for five dollars.
