// THE LOOP

The plan was simple on paper: leave Liberec on a Sunday, drive west into France, loop south through the Iberian Peninsula, and come back along the Mediterranean coast. Seven thousand kilometres. Four people, one car, eleven days. The route made a rough arc on the map — northwest first, into Champagne and Normandy, then down the Atlantic side and across the Pyrenees, east along the Spanish coast, and home through Provence and the Dolomites. Every stop had something broken on it: a runway no one lands on anymore, a fortress no one escaped from, a skyscraper no one could put a lift in. We went looking for those.

// FRANCE — BRIENNE AIR BASE · CHÂTEAU D'ÉPERNON · PARIS

FR · Champagne → Île-de-France · Aug 8

The first stop, eight hundred kilometres from home, is a decommissioned NATO airfield in Champagne. Vitry-Brienne is not a ruin exactly — the fence is still up, the runway is still flat — but the aircraft parked at the edge of the grass haven't moved in decades. A Lockheed Neptune, number 563, French Navy markings still readable on the fuselage, sits in the weeds. The propellers have turned their last. The undercarriage has sunk slightly into the soil. It is the kind of object that becomes beautiful when it stops being useful.

Forty minutes south, near Fontenay-Trésigny, the Château du duc d'Épernon has been standing since the 12th century. It was rebuilt under Charles VI, rebuilt again under Jean-Louis de Nogaret in the 17th century into something fit for the royal court, and then left to slowly fall apart. The upper floors are open to the sky now; the roof timbers have collapsed inward, exposing a cathedral of rotting beams and graffiti. The walls still hold. Listed as a historical monument, the château was recently acquired by a restoration firm — the park is being opened to visitors, scaffolding is probably somewhere inside by now. We caught it in the window between derelict and reconstructed.

We hit Paris after dark. The Eiffel Tower does its light show on the hour. We watched it once and drove on.

// FRANCE — POINTE DU HOC & OMAHA BEACH

FR · Normandy · Aug 9

The Normandy coast in August doesn't guarantee summer — the sky was low and grey, the grass on the clifftop wet, the Channel invisible behind the mist. None of that diminishes the place. The bomb craters are still there, eighty years on, as wide and deep as the day they were made. American Rangers scaled these cliffs on the morning of June 6, 1944, to destroy guns that weren't even there yet — the Germans had moved them inland. The Rangers took the point anyway.

The bunkers are open to walk through. Low concrete ceilings, rusting rebar bleeding through the walls, doorways that frame nothing but grey light. The Germans built them to last. They did.

Down the coast at Omaha Beach, the Les Braves memorial stands in the sand at the waterline — dark steel elements rising like spread wings or open petals, three groups representing hope, freedom, and fraternity. In the rain it looked like something that had grown there. Behind it, a commemorative plaque in the wet stone. The tide was out. Nobody else was around.

// FRANCE — LANDÉVENNEC SHIP GRAVEYARD

FR · Brittany · Aug 9–10

The French Navy keeps its decommissioned ships in the Aulne estuary, behind the abbey at Landévennec. On the evening of the 9th we found the viewpoint above the river — four or five grey hulls moored in the fog, the wooded hills coming down to the water on both sides. The kind of photograph that needs no filter.

The next morning we were on board. The ships sit low in the water; the decks are stripped of anything useful, rails rusting, hatches open to the sky. From the bow of one you can see the others lined up in the inlet, and the river winding back toward the sea. The chain anchor had gone orange. The water underneath was very green.

// SPAIN — PYRENEES: PAMPLONA, ESCO, CANFRANC

ES · Aragón / Navarre

Fort San Cristóbal sits on the hill above Pamplona. We arrived after dark on the 11th and climbed to the top — the city was spread below in a grid of orange lights, the fort a dark mass against the sky above it. In May 1938, 795 Republican prisoners attempted a mass escape from here, the largest prison break in Spanish history. Most were recaptured within days, shot, or drowned crossing the Bidasoa river into France. Two made it. The fort is now officially a bat sanctuary and a protected heritage site.

Inside, the cells run in long vaulted corridors, headlamps only. The scale is larger than expected — a working fortress first, a prison second, built in 1890 and expanded by Franco for political prisoners during and after the Civil War. The Spanish coat of arms and old slogans still legible on the walls above the tunnel arches.

The same day we drove east to Esco, a village partially flooded when the Yesa reservoir was built. The water rose, the people left, and the stone houses have been slowly reclaimed by the hillside ever since. Some walls have been restored; most haven't. Then further into the Pyrenees to Canfranc. The main station building is being converted into a hotel — a glass-and-iron palace opened in 1928 to link Spain with France, closed in 1970 after a bridge accident on the French side, and now getting a second life. But right next to the tunnel entrance, a few metres from the construction hoardings, an old maintenance depot sits untouched. Passenger cars with wooden-panelled corridors, compartment doors hanging open, the ceiling peeling in sheets. A rusted freight wagon half-buried in the gravel outside. The tunnel mouth dark behind it.

// SPAIN — LA BARRANCA SANATORIUM & MADRID

ES · Sierra de Guadarrama → Madrid · Aug 13

La Barranca sits in the pine forest above the Sierra de Guadarrama, an hour north of Madrid. The sanatorium complex was built in the 1940s to treat tuberculosis patients — multiple large buildings scattered through the trees, connected by covered walkways that are mostly gone now. The dry heat hit as soon as we got out of the car. The ground cracked, the grass yellow, the building walls still white against a blue sky with no clouds in it. Inside the main block the wards were stripped bare — no furniture, no fittings, just concrete floors and open windows looking into the pines. The place has the specific emptiness of somewhere that was very full once.

Madrid in August is almost absurdly hot. The city empties of those who can afford to leave; the rest go out at night when the temperature drops to something survivable. We arrived in the afternoon, found a rooftop with a view over the whole basin of the city, and stayed until the sun got low. In the streets below, a parade was moving through the centre — a pirate ship float covered in rainbow balloons, costumed figures, music, masked onlookers filming on their phones. It was the day before the Verbena de la Paloma, Madrid's biggest summer festival, and the neighbourhood comparsa groups were already in the streets. We joined for a block and then peeled off for dinner.

// SPAIN — ANDALUSIA: TABERNAS & CABO DE GATA

ES · Almería · Aug 14

The Tabernas desert is the only true desert in continental Europe — 280 square kilometres of eroded badlands between the Sierra Nevada and the coast. Sergio Leone filmed here. So did David Lean, Ridley Scott, and dozens of others who needed a landscape that looked like nowhere specific. The white houses of Tabernas village sit at the base of the cliffs, terracotta roofs, laundry in the wind. The rock above is red and layered and doesn't look entirely real. On the other side of the same cliff face, old cave dwellings are cut directly into the hillside — low vaulted ceilings carved from the stone, leaf debris on the floor, a single opening letting in the light.

In the evening we drove down to Cabo de Gata, found a rocky beach below the headland, and swam. The sun went down behind the hills while we were still in the water. We slept somewhere nearby and left the Rodalquilar mine for the morning.

// SPAIN — RODALQUILAR, INTEMPO & TORRETA DE MIRAMAR

ES · Almería → Benidorm · Aug 15

Rodalquilar, near Cabo de Gata, is where they extracted gold from the 1940s until the mine became uneconomical in 1966. The processing plant is still standing — circular settling tanks, concrete towers and conveyors — rusting against dry mountains. The surrounding hills have been turned inside out by decades of excavation. In the morning light, before the heat built up, it was very quiet.

Then north, several hours up the coast to Benidorm. The Intempo skyscraper is 192 metres tall, one of the tallest residential buildings in Europe — and was built without a functioning lift plan for the upper floors. Construction stalled in the 2008 crash, restarted, stalled again. From the top the whole bay is visible, the rocky Peñón de Benidorm sitting in turquoise water below. Someone had left a hard hat on a beam. The Torreta de Miramar we found after dark — a lone concrete tower standing in an empty lot on the edge of a residential area, lit orange from the street lights below, city spreading out around it.

// SPAIN — CATALONIA: SITGES, BARCELONA, TRES CHIMENEAS

ES · Catalunya · Aug 16–17

Ocio Aventura Sitges was an adventure park — waterslides, climbing structures, the usual — and has been abandoned long enough for the concrete to have turned grey and the paint to have mostly gone. The big slide is still intact. You can sit on the rim at the top and watch the sea. Barcelona was everything after that: the Gòtic quarter, the cathedral, the crowds, the heat bouncing off the stone. We found a bar and stayed until it cooled down.

The Festa Major de Gràcia runs every August, each street in the neighbourhood decorated by its residents — handmade installations, coloured lights, competing for best-dressed alley. We walked through after dark on the 17th: one street hung with glowing jellyfish, another with illuminated clouds, people sitting out at tables underneath. It is a genuinely good festival. The Tres Chimeneas — the towering chimneys of the old Sant Adrià de Besòs thermal plant — we passed on the way out of the city, lit up against the night sky above the beach.

// FRANCE — MARSEILLE & TOULON

FR · Provence · Aug 18–19

Marseille has been covered in Space Invader mosaics since the late 1990s — the anonymous street artist has placed hundreds of small ceramic tile aliens across the city's facades, corners, and stairwells, each one documented and mapped online. The game is to find them. We found several. Notre-Dame de la Garde sits above the whole port on its hill, gold Madonna on the tower, the city and the Mediterranean spread out below. It has been watching ships come and go since 1864. Nothing much has changed from up there.

Toulon is a military port — the Duquesne, a French guided-missile destroyer, was moored at the quayside and decommissioned. D603. From the outside it looks like any warship: grey, angular, bristling with equipment. From the inside it is a maze of low corridors, watertight doors, and engine rooms the size of apartment blocks. The main turbines sit in the dark, insulated pipes running in every direction, graffiti on the casings. It smelled of fuel and sealed air.

// ITALY — LAGO DI LANDRO

IT · Dolomites · Aug 19–20

The last stop before home. Lago di Landro sits in a valley in the Dolomites, the Cristallo massif to the south, the road running right along the shore. We stopped in the evening when the light was low and the water was flat and the peaks were reflecting in it. The drive east from there through the Austrian border and into Czechia took the rest of the night. Liberec in the morning. End of loop.
